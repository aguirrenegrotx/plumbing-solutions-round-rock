Leak Repair in Round Rock, TX

Leak repair for dripping fixtures, wet cabinets, and pipe joints in Round Rock.

Call (737) 405-5790 Ask a question

A drip from a faucet can seem minor until the sink cabinet stays damp, the wood swells around the supply valve, or water reaches the floor beneath it. A pinhole in a copper line may leave only a small stain at first, then spread moisture through drywall, baseboards, insulation, or a ceiling below. The longer water travels behind a wall or under a vanity, the harder it is to see where the original leak began.

A leak can also change with the season and with daily water use. A loose compression nut may only seep while a faucet runs, while a failed toilet supply line or refrigerator line can keep feeding water into a cabinet even when nobody is using the fixture. Mineral buildup around a valve stem, a wet ring below a pipe joint, and a water meter that moves when fixtures are off can all point to water leaving the plumbing system.

An active leak, a wet ceiling, a soft cabinet floor, or water near an electrical outlet should not wait because the water is already reaching building materials beyond the fitting itself. A contained faucet drip may be scheduled, but we still recommend repair before the washer, cartridge, or valve body creates damage around the fixture.

Dripping faucets and wet cabinets

Customers often call us about a faucet that will not stop dripping, a sink cabinet with water under the pipes, or a puddle that returns after the cabinet has been dried. A drip at the spout can come from a worn cartridge, a damaged washer, or a valve seat, while water around the base of the faucet can be coming from a loose mounting nut, failed gasket, or a supply connection under the counter.

A hissing sound behind drywall, a brown spot on a ceiling, peeling paint near a shower wall, or a warm patch on a floor can indicate a concealed water line leak. Some customers notice a musty smell in a vanity cabinet, while others see the water meter continue to move after faucets, ice makers, and other fixtures are shut off.

Water around an angle stop, corrosion on a braided supply line, and a damp pipe joint can look alike from the front of a cabinet. These symptoms can come from separate parts and require different repairs, so we inspect the connection and water path before tying the work to a particular part or repair scope.

Fixture and pipe inspection

We start by locating the wet area and tracing the water path back to the fixture, valve, supply line, drain connection, or pipe joint. We check visible fittings with a light, inspect cabinet panels and drywall with a moisture meter, and run the affected fixture to see whether the leak appears during use, after use, or continuously. Where the leak is not visible, we can isolate fixtures and use the water meter or a pressure gauge to narrow down the source.

A typical residential water supply is commonly regulated between 40 and 80 psi, and unusually high pressure can stress faucet cartridges, supply hoses, and shutoff valves. For a suspected hidden supply leak, an isolated pressure test may be watched for 15 minutes or longer while fixtures remain off. An accessible faucet, trap connection, or under-sink supply line can often be checked in 30 to 60 minutes, although damp drywall or a concealed pipe can require additional tracing.

On the same visit, we can commonly replace exposed washers, cartridges, supply lines, escutcheons, or leaking compression fittings when the correct part is available and the connection is accessible. A pipe behind tile, inside a wall, under a slab, or behind a built-in cabinet may need an access opening, a specialty fitting, or a follow-up visit after parts are obtained. We explain which pipe or fitting is leaking before work moves beyond the visible area.

Shutoff valves and wall access

The condition of the shutoff valve and the route to the leak are major factors in the repair scope. A supply line connected to a working angle stop inside an open sink cabinet is different from a line whose shutoff has seized, whose packing nut is corroded, or whose valve must be replaced before the leaking connection can be safely worked on. Water may need to be isolated at another valve when the local shutoff will not close.

Wall access also changes the work involved. A leak at an exposed PEX, copper, or PVC joint can be repaired directly, while a pipe behind tile, plaster, a shower enclosure, cabinetry, or insulation may require a careful opening and later surface repair by the appropriate trade. The age and material of the fitting matter because older valves, galvanized pipe, discontinued faucet cartridges, and specialty hose connections can need ordered parts rather than a standard replacement.

We also account for whether the leak has affected a shared wall, a ceiling cavity, or a location where building approval or property management sign-off is required. A clearly visible fitting with open access is generally more straightforward than a concealed line with damaged material around it, and we discuss those conditions before expanding the repair.

Fixture room and valve model

Before calling, note which room and fixture are involved, such as the kitchen sink, upstairs bathroom vanity, shower valve, laundry box, or exterior hose bib. If the faucet or shower trim has a brand name or model marking on the handle, escutcheon plate, spout, or underside of the fixture, that marking can help us identify the cartridge or replacement part.

Tell us whether water is running now, whether the leak only appears while the fixture is on, and whether the local shutoff valve closes. A photo of the wet cabinet, supply hose, pipe joint, or ceiling stain can show the access around the fitting before we arrive.

If the fixture was installed recently or has an approximate age, include that with the location of any access panel, crawlspace entry, or wall opening.
