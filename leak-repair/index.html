Leak Repair in Round Rock, TX

We trace and repair active pipe, valve, and hidden-water leaks in Round Rock.

Call (737) 405-5790 Ask a question

A drip beneath a sink and a wet spot spreading across a hallway ceiling are both often called a leak, but they are not the same job. A visible drip usually leaves water at a fixture, valve, or supply connection; a hidden leak may show up as a ceiling stain, soft drywall, peeling paint, or a water meter that moves while every tap is off. We trace both the exposed source and the pipe route behind the finished surface.

We check plumbing leaks at kitchens, bathrooms, laundry rooms, garages, ceilings, walls, and outdoor hose connections throughout Round Rock, Brushy Creek, Windemere, and Wells Branch. The source is not always directly above or beside the damp area, since water can run along a pipe, floor joist, cabinet base, or drywall seam before it becomes visible.

An old, dry stain can sometimes wait until the area is inspected and the source is confirmed, but active water, a growing stain, a hissing pipe, or a moving meter should not wait. Water from a pressurized line can soak insulation, swell cabinet panels, damage drywall, and keep feeding a concealed leak even when the visible puddle is small.

Water stain and hissing

Customers often call about a brown ceiling spot, a hissing sound inside a wall, water collecting under a cabinet, or damp carpet along a baseboard. Other common signs include a warm patch on a tile floor, paint bubbling near a shower wall, a musty odor in a closet, or a meter dial that continues to turn after faucets and appliances are off. Each symptom points us toward a different section of pipe, fitting, valve, or drain connection.

A ceiling stain may come from a bathroom supply line above it, while water under a vanity may come from an angle stop, a trap connection, or a split supply hose. A hissing sound can indicate pressurized water escaping, whereas a slow cabinet drip may appear only when a fixture is used. These faults can look similar from the room, but the repair materials, access work, and labor involved are different once we inspect the source.

Pressure test and inspection

During a leak inspection, we start with the visible water path, then check nearby shutoff valves, supply lines, pipe joints, fixture connections, cabinet bases, drywall, and the water meter. We can isolate sections of plumbing, check static and flowing water pressure, and look for pressure changes that point to a supply-side leak. Residential water pressure is generally kept at or below 80 psi; excessive pressure can put added strain on valve seals, braided hoses, and older pipe connections.

A straightforward source trace often takes 30 to 60 minutes when the pipe or fitting is accessible. If we locate an exposed leaking supply line, angle stop, valve, pipe section, or connector and have the appropriate replacement material, that repair is usually handled during the same visit. Repairs requiring a wall opening, ceiling access, a shutoff that will not close, a specialty valve, or a replacement part that must be ordered are scheduled as follow-up work after we identify the route and material.

Pipe access and shutoff valves

The condition of the leaking part affects the scope of repair. A newer braided connector or exposed valve may be replaced without disturbing finished surfaces, while an aging copper joint, galvanized pipe, or damaged line behind tile can require more removal and restoration around the pipe. The material, fitting type, and route from the shutoff to the leak all matter when we determine the repair needed.

Access also changes the work. A cabinet back, access panel, crawlspace opening, ceiling cavity, or wall section may provide a direct path to the line, while a pipe behind a shower surround or finished ceiling requires more strip-back. A seized shutoff valve can prevent isolation at the fixture, and a specialty cartridge, valve body, or matching pipe fitting may need to be ordered before the repair can be completed. If the work involves a permitted alteration or inspection sign-off, that requirement is addressed before the affected plumbing is closed back up.

Fixture plate and access panel

Before calling, note which room has the water and which fixture or wall is closest to the stain, puddle, or sound. If a valve, faucet, appliance connection, or access cover has a make, model, or part number stamped on the body or printed on a label, a clear photo can help us identify the fitting. Let us know whether water is actively running now, whether the main shutoff or local shutoff still operates, and whether the area is behind a cabinet, ceiling, drywall panel, or crawlspace opening. If you know when the fixture or supply line was installed, that age can also help narrow down likely seals, hoses, and valve parts.
