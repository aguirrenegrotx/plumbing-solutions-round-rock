Faucet Repair in Round Rock, TX

We repair dripping, loose, noisy, and leaking faucets across Round Rock.

Call (737) 405-5790 Ask a question

We hear from homeowners when the kitchen faucet “won’t shut off,” the bathroom handle has gone loose, or there is “a little puddle” beneath the sink cabinet. Those descriptions matter because a faucet can leak from the spout, around the handle, below the escutcheon plate, or at a supply connection, and each location points us toward a different part.

A drip from the spout usually leads us to the cartridge, stem, washer, or valve seat. Water collecting around the handle can come from worn O-rings or a damaged cartridge seal. A wet cabinet floor may involve the faucet base, mounting hardware, supply line, or shutoff valve rather than the spout itself, so we inspect the fixture before deciding which repair fits.

A faucet that gives an occasional drip can generally be planned for repair, but a faucet that runs continuously, leaks into a cabinet, or will not turn off should not wait. Water from a faucet leak can damage the cabinet base, loosen mounting hardware, stain the countertop edge, and keep the water meter moving even when no fixture is being used.

Dripping tap and loose handle

“My faucet keeps dripping” is one of the most common calls, whether the drops come from a kitchen spout or a bathroom basin faucet after the handle is off. We also hear that the handle turns too far, spins without changing the water flow, squeaks when moved, or has become hard to turn. A whistling or chattering sound can show up when water passes a worn cartridge, clogged aerator, loose washer, or restricted supply connection.

Another call starts with water appearing at the counter around the faucet base, on the sink rim, or inside the vanity cabinet. A weak stream from one faucet, uneven hot and cold flow, and a spray pattern that shoots sideways are separate symptoms as well. These faults can look similar from across the room, but the cartridge, O-rings, aerator, mounting seal, and supply connection are different repairs with different parts and labor involved.

Fixture inspection

At the fixture, we begin by running the hot and cold sides, watching the spout, handle, base, and cabinet connections for the leak path. We check the handle movement, identify the faucet make where possible, inspect the aerator for sediment, and test the angle stops before isolating water. When flow or pressure is part of the complaint, we can compare the faucet stream with another fixture and use a gauge where needed; many residential plumbing systems operate in the range of 40 to 80 psi.

A straightforward faucet inspection and common repair often takes 30 to 90 minutes, depending on access to the nuts, supply lines, and shutoff valves. We remove the handle and trim, inspect the cartridge or stem, and replace worn seals, O-rings, a cartridge, or an aerator when the correct component is available. We also tighten the mounting hardware and test the spout and cabinet area after water is restored. If a seized shutoff, corroded supply line, damaged valve body, discontinued cartridge, or replacement fixture requires a special part, we explain what needs to be ordered and arrange the remaining work for another visit.

Cartridge access and shutoffs

The age and type of cartridge are major factors in a faucet repair. A common cartridge, washer, or aerator is usually simpler to identify than a discontinued component or a specialty finish with a brand-specific valve body. A faucet that has been dripping for a long time can also have mineral buildup on the retaining nut, stem, or valve seat, making the strip-down more involved than a recent handle leak.

Access under the sink affects the work as much as the visible faucet does. A clear cabinet with working angle stops allows the fixture to be isolated and tested directly, while a seized shutoff, corroded supply connection, crowded vanity, or damaged cabinet floor adds work before the faucet itself can be repaired. If the valve body is cracked, the mounting deck is damaged, a replacement fixture must be ordered, or building requirements call for sign-off, those conditions change the scope from a component repair to a larger fixture job.

Model number and fixture

Before calling, note which room and which faucet has the problem, such as the kitchen sink, primary bathroom basin, laundry sink, or bar sink. If you have the installation sheet, box, receipt, or a tag from the fixture, the model number and make can help us identify the correct cartridge or handle parts. A clear photo of the spout, handles, logo, and area beneath the sink is useful when the model number is not available. Let us know roughly how old the faucet is, whether water is running now, and whether the cabinet has clear access to the shutoff valves. Mention any wobble at the faucet base or water on the cabinet floor so we can prepare for the mounting hardware and connections involved.
