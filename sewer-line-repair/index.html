Sewer Line Repair in Round Rock, TX

Camera-led sewer line checks and repair planning for backups, odors, and slow fixtures.

Call (737) 405-5790 Ask a question

We hear from homeowners and property managers when “everything is backing up,” “the shower drain is bubbling,” or “it smells like sewage outside.” Those are the words people use because the trouble often shows up first at a toilet bowl, tub drain, shower, or floor drain rather than at the buried pipe itself. We translate the symptom into a sewer-line check, looking for a blockage, root intrusion, cracked pipe, separated joint, or a line that no longer has a clear route to the connection.

A single slow sink can be limited to one fixture, but water rising in a shower drain when another fixture runs points further down the system. We work across Round Rock, Brushy Creek, Windemere, and Wells Branch to inspect the line and identify the section that needs attention. A sewer backup should not wait, because wastewater can come through low drains, soak flooring and wall materials, and continue forcing solids into a damaged or blocked pipe.

Sewage smell and gurgling

People commonly call when a toilet bubbles after the washing machine drains, a shower pan fills with murky water, or several drains slow down at once. A sewage smell near the yard, cleanout, or bathroom can also indicate a loose cap, leaking joint, or a buried line that is allowing wastewater to escape. Another frequent report is a wet strip of ground above the pipe route, especially when the soil stays damp without recent rain.

A gurgling bowl can come from restricted flow, while water at a shower drain may mean the main line cannot carry discharge away. Roots entering a joint, scale inside older cast-iron pipe, a belly in the line, and a collapsed section can all create similar household symptoms. These complaints sound alike from inside the home, but the pipe fault, repair method, materials, and work involved can be very different once we inspect the route.

Camera inspection and locating

At the visit, we start with the accessible cleanout, drain openings, and the fixtures affected by the backup. We check whether water is standing in the pipe, whether the cleanout cap can be removed safely, and whether flow changes when a fixture is used. A camera inspection sends a flexible cable through the line, while a transmitter helps us trace the pipe route from above ground. A straightforward camera pass commonly takes about fifteen to thirty minutes; tracing a long route, locating an underground defect, or working through limited access can take longer.

The camera shows the pipe material, joint condition, root growth, offset connections, cracks, and the approximate location of a restriction. Residential building drains are often three or four inches in diameter, and that measurement matters when planning a replacement fitting or pipe section. When a cracked cleanout riser, cap, or exposed pipe section is accessible, we may be able to replace it during the same visit. Excavation, replacement of a longer buried run, restoration of concrete or landscaping, and any repair requiring permit review are typically planned as follow-up work after the line location and scope are clear.

Pipe depth and access

Pipe depth and access change the scope of a sewer repair more than the symptom inside the bathroom. A defect beneath open soil beside a driveway is different from one beneath a concrete slab, patio, fence line, mature tree, or utility route. We also consider whether the cleanout provides a usable entry point, whether the pipe can be reached from the yard, and whether wastewater must be controlled before work begins.

The pipe material and failure type matter as well. Root intrusion at a joint, a split in plastic pipe, corrosion in cast iron, and a crushed section each call for different fittings and repair methods. A line with a known route and an accessible damaged section is generally more direct to plan than a buried route that needs locating, excavation, ordered material, or local permit and inspection sign-off.

Cleanout location and pipe material

Before calling, we can use the location of the cleanout and the room where the backup first appeared to plan the inspection. The cleanout is often a capped pipe near an exterior wall, foundation, or yard line; please do not force a cap that is stuck or surrounded by standing wastewater. If you know whether the pipe is cast iron, PVC, ABS, or clay, tell us, and mention any prior excavation, patch, or visible pipe section.

We also need to know which fixtures are backing up, whether water is currently standing in a shower, tub, or floor drain, and whether the yard, crawlspace, driveway, or gate provides access to the suspected route. Photos of the cleanout, wet ground, or exposed pipe can help us understand the layout before the visit.
